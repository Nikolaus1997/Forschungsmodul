\begin{abstract}
	The porous medium equation (PME) is a nonlinear partial differential equation of parabolic type, widely used to model processes such as fluid flow, heat transfer, and diffusion in porous media. This research project employs the Local Discontinuous Galerkin (LDG) method to numerically solve the PME. The method is evaluated under various configurations, including different polynomial orders of basis functions, to assess their impact on solution behavior. In addition, a Hyperbolic Balance Law (HBL) system, known to degenerate into the PME under appropriate parameter regimes, is investigated. For both numerical approaches, extensive simulations are conducted to explore their respective strengths and limitations. The results demonstrate that the HBL formulation qualitatively captures the essential features of nonlinear diffusion and is capable of handling certain non-smooth initial data in regimes with relatively small nonlinearity.
\end{abstract}

	
	\section{Introduction}
The \text{porous medium equation (PME)} is a nonlinear diffusion equation that models various physical and biological phenomena, such as isentropic gas flow through porous media, nonlinear heat conduction. The unknown function \( u(x,t) \) typically represents a nonnegative quantity such as concentration, density, or temperature.\\
The general form of the PME is
\[
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \Delta (u^m), \quad x \in \Omega, \quad t > 0,
\]
where \( m > 1 \) is a constant characterizing the medium's diffusion behavior. Assuming that the initial condition \( u_0 \) is nonnegative, the PME can be rewritten as
\[
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \Delta (u^m) = \nabla \cdot (m u^{m-1} \nabla u) = \nabla \cdot (a(u)\nabla u), \quad x \in \Omega, \quad t > 0,
\]
where \( a(u) = m u^{m-1} \geq 0 \). Generally, the PME can be identified as a nonlinear diffusion equation of the degenerate parabolic type, since it degenerates when \( u=0 \). Notably, if the initial data has compact support, the solution of the initial value problem for the PME will also have compact support at any given time \( t>0 \). Due to this feature, the PME exhibits finite speed of propagation at the moving front or ``free boundary'' region where the solution $ u $ vanishes.\\ Another interesting novelty of the PME is the so-called waiting time phenomenon, which can occur for specially chosen initial data. In this case, the free boundary remains stationary until a certain time (the waiting time), after which it instantaneously moves with finite speed, e.g. see chapter \ref{sec:numerical-results}.\\
A fundamental example of a solution is the Barenblatt (or source-type) solution \cite{Barenblatt}, which can be written explicitly as the formula 
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:barenblatt function}
	B_m(x, t)=t^{-k}\left[\left(1-\frac{k(m-1)}{2 m} \frac{|x|^2}{t^{2 k}}\right)_{+}\right]^{1 /(m-1)},
\end{equation}
where \( (u)_{+}=\max (u, 0) \) and \( k=1/(m+1) \). For any time \( t>0 \), this solution has compact support \(\left[-\alpha_m(t), \alpha_m(t)\right]\), with the interface/free boundary moving outward at finite speed $\alpha_m(t)$, where
\[
\alpha_m(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2 m}{k(m-1)}} \cdot t^k.
\]
This solution will later be useful for assessing the accuracy of the numerical methods presented. In this work, two numerical frameworks will be tested. One relies on the Local Discontinuous Galerkin (LDG) formulation proposed by Zhang and Wu \cite{Zhang2009}, while the other employs a hyperbolic system that degenerates into the PME as the parameter \(\varepsilon \to 0\), similar to the system introduced by Jin, Pareschi, and Toscani \cite{Jin1998}. Both approaches utilize a Galerkin ansatz on discontinuous basis functions of varying polynomial degree. Since this ansatz tends to produce oscillations near the free boundary, the LDG method introduces an artificial diffusion parameter \(\gamma\) and employs limiters extensively in the vicinity of the free boundary to address this issue. In contrast, for the hyperbolic system, these oscillations are unavoidable, as will be demonstrated in chapter \ref{sec:numerical-results}.

\section{Mathematical and Numerical Formulation}
To numerically solve the porous medium equation (PME), we discretize the spatial domain using a finite number of non-overlapping cells and apply a high-order accurate numerical method tailored for problems with nonlinear diffusion and potential degeneracies. Specifically, we employ the Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method, known for its flexibility in handling complex boundary conditions, its ability to use high-order polynomial approximations, and its local conservation properties.\\
\\
The computational domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}$ is divided into \( N_{\text{cells}} \in \mathbb{N} \) cells, where each cell
\[
I_i = \left(x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}, x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\right)
\]
has width 
\[
h_i = x_{i+\frac{1}{2}} - x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}, \quad i = 1, \ldots, N_{\text{cells}}.
\]For notation purposes $x_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}$ denotes the position at the interfaces and $x_i$ denotes the mid point of the cell $i$.\\
Since we use the DG approach, we adopt an ansatz that separates spatial and temporal dependencies of the solution. Within each cell \( I_i \), the approximate solutions are represented as
\[
u_h(x,t)\big|_{I_i} = \sum_{l=0}^n u_i^l(t)\,\varphi_h^l(x), \quad
j_h(x,t)\big|_{I_i} = \sum_{l=0}^n j_i^l(t)\,\varphi_h^l(x), \quad
q_h(x,t)\big|_{I_i} = \sum_{l=0}^n q_i^l(t)\,\varphi_h^l(x),
\]
with $j,\,q$ the auxiliary variables (see chapter \ref{sec:LDG} and \ref{sec:HBL}). The basis functions \(\varphi_h^l\) are scaled Legendre polynomials defined by
\[
\varphi_h^l(x) = p_l\left(\frac{2(x - x_i)}{h_i}\right), \quad x \in I_i,
\]
and \( p_l \) denotes the Legendre polynomial of degree  \( l \in \mathbb{N}_0 \) and $\frac{2(x - x_i)}{h_i}\in [-1,1]$ is the scaled argument in terms of the current cell width.
For the initial solution \( u_h(0) \), we use the \( L^2 \)-projection of the given initial data \( u_0 \) into the discrete function space
\[
V_h^n = \left\{ v_h : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \;\middle|\; v_h|_{I_i} \in \mathcal{P}_n(I_i), \forall i=1,\ldots,N_{\text{cells}} \right\},
\]
where \( \mathcal{P}_n(I_i) \) denotes the space of polynomials of degree at most \( n \) on the cell \( I_i \). The initial projection satisfies
\[
\int_{I_i} \left(u_h(0,x) - u_0(x)\right) \varphi_h(x) \, dx = 0, \quad \forall \varphi_h \in V_h^n, \quad \forall i=1,\ldots,N_{\text{cells}}.
\]
The Legendre polynomials and their derivatives are computed using\\ Algorithm 22: \texttt{LegendrePolynomialAndDerivative} from Kopriva \cite{Kopriva2009}. To accurately evaluate integrals, we employ Gauss–Legendre quadrature with nodes and weights obtained from Algorithm 23: \texttt{LegendreGaussNodesAndWeights} (also from Kopriva), allowing for flexible polynomial degrees.

\subsection{The Local Discontinuous Galerkin (LDG) Method}\label{sec:LDG}
A common strategy for addressing the nonlinearity in the porous medium equation (PME) is to introduce auxiliary variables, which facilitate the construction of a Local Discontinuous Galerkin (LDG) scheme, as proposed by Cockburn and Shu~\cite{CockburnandShuLDG}. By carefully selecting appropriate numerical fluxes at element interfaces, the resulting formulation has been proven to be \( L^2 \)-stable (see Chapter 2.3 in~\cite{CockburnandShuLDG}).\\
\\
To apply the LDG method, we first rewrite the PME in first-order form by introducing auxiliary variables \( g \) and \( q \):

\begin{align*}
	\partial_t u + \partial_x\left[f(u) - \sqrt{a(u)}\,q\right] &= s(u), &x \in I,~t > 0, \\
	q - \partial_x g &= 0, &x \in I,~t > 0,
\end{align*}
where
\[
g(u) = \int_0^u \sqrt{a(s)}\,ds, \quad \text{with} \quad a(u) = m u^{m-1}.
\]
Following the proposed LDG approach by Cockburn and Shu, Zhang and Wu~\cite{Zhang2009} introduced the following numerical fluxes to handle discontinuities at element interfaces. In conjunction with the spatial DG discretization, the semi-discrete formulation in a cell \( I_i \) reads as:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\partial_t u_i^l(t) &= \frac{2l+1}{h} \int_{I_i} \left[ s(u_h) \varphi^l(x) + \left( f(u_h) - \sqrt{a(u_h)}\,q_h \right) \partial_x \varphi^l(x) \right]\,dx \\
		&\quad - \frac{2l+1}{h} \left[ \left( \hat{f}(u_h) - \frac{\llbracket g(u_h) \rrbracket}{\llbracket u_h \rrbracket} \mean{q_h} - \gamma \llbracket q_h \rrbracket \right) \varphi^l(x) \right]_{x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}^{x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}, \\
		q_i^l(t) &= -\frac{2l+1}{h} \int_{I_i} g(u_h) \partial_x \varphi^l(x)\,dx + \left[ \left( -\mean{g(u_h)} + \gamma \llbracket u_h \rrbracket \right) \varphi^l(x) \right]_{x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}^{x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}},
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:semidiscreteLDG}
\end{equation}
where the arithmetic mean and jump operators are defined at cell interfaces as:
\[
\mean{g(u_h)} = \frac{g(u_h^+) + g(u_h^-)}{2}, \quad
\llbracket u_h \rrbracket = u_h^+ - u_h^-.
\]
When \( \llbracket u_h \rrbracket \neq 0 \), the term
\begin{equation}
\frac{\llbracket g(u_h) \rrbracket}{\llbracket u_h \rrbracket}
\label{eq:jumpterm}
\end{equation}
is well-defined and serves as an approximation of \( \sqrt{a(u_h)} \). However, when \( \llbracket u_h \rrbracket = 0 \), this expression becomes undefined. To preserve numerical stability and ensure a meaningful evaluation in such cases, it is customary to replace the undefined ratio with
\[
\sqrt{a(u_h)}.
\]
The numerical behavior of this replacement is strongly influenced by how equality is determined between \( u_h^+ \) and \( u_h^- \). To robustly assess whether these two values are approximately equal within machine precision, we adopt the adaptive tolerance criterion proposed by Kopriva~\cite{Kopriva2009}, specifically Algorithm 139: \texttt{AlmostEqual}. This method enhances the stability and reliability of the scheme by applying a practical and adaptive threshold for floating-point comparisons.

\subsection{Hyperbolic Balance law System}\label{sec:HBL}
Another approach to numerically solve the porous medium equation is to consider a hyperbolic system that degenerates to the porous medium equation as \( \varepsilon \to 0 \), similar to the method proposed by Jin, Pareschi, and Toscani in \cite{Jin1998}. 
To this end, we introduce an auxiliary variable \( \mathbf{j} \) and a relaxation parameter \( \varepsilon \), leading to the following system of hyperbolic balance laws:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\partial_t u^{\varepsilon} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{j}^{\varepsilon} &= 0, \\
		\varepsilon \partial_t \mathbf{j}^{\varepsilon} + \nabla \left( \left( u^{\varepsilon} \right)^m \right) &= -\mathbf{j}^{\varepsilon}.
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
As already mentioned, we use for the spatial discretization the Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method incorporation with scaled Legendre polynomials as basis functions. This yields the following semi-discrete formulation given for cell \( I_i \)
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{aligned}
		\int_{I_i} \partial_t u_h(x,t) \varphi^r(x) \, dx 
		&+ \int_{I_i} \partial_x j_h(x,t) \varphi^r(x) \, dx = 0, \\
		\int_{I_i} \varepsilon \, \partial_t j_h(x,t) \varphi^r(x) \, dx 
		&+ \int_{I_i} \partial_x (u_h^m) \, \varphi^r(x) \, dx = -\int_{I_i} j_h \, \varphi^r(x) \, dx.
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Projecting onto the Legendre basis and exploiting orthogonality, we obtain the following system of ODEs for the modal coefficients \( u_i^l(t) \) and \( j_i^l(t) \):
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{aligned}
		\int_{I_i} \partial_t \left( \sum_{l=0}^{n} u_i^l(t) \varphi^l(x) \right) \varphi^r(x) \, dx 
		&+ \int_{I_i} \partial_x \left( \sum_{l=0}^{n} j_i^l(t) \varphi^l(x) \right) \varphi^r(x) \, dx = 0, \\
		\int_{I_i} \varepsilon \, \partial_t \left( \sum_{l=0}^{n} j_i^l(t) \varphi^l(x) \right) \varphi^r(x) \, dx 
		&+ \int_{I_i} \partial_x (u_h^m) \, \varphi^r(x) \, dx = -\int_{I_i} j_h \, \varphi^r(x) \, dx.
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
$\Leftrightarrow$
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\partial_t u_i^l(t) &= \frac{2l+1}{h_i} \int_{I_i} j_h \partial_x\varphi_h^l  \, dx 
		- \frac{2l+1}{h_i} \left[ \widehat{j}_h \varphi_h^l \right]_{x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}^{x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}, \\
		\varepsilon \, \partial_t j_i^l(t) &= -\frac{2l+1}{h_i} \int_{I_i} j_h \, \varphi_h^l \, dx 
		+ \frac{2l+1}{h_i} \int_{I_i} (u_h)^m \partial_x\varphi_h^l\, dx 
		- \frac{2l+1}{h_i} \left[ \widehat{u}^m_h \varphi_h^l \right]_{x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}^{x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}},
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:semidiscreteHP}
\end{equation}
where \( \widehat{j}_h \) and \( \widehat{u}_h \) are appropriate numerical fluxes defined at the cell interfaces to ensure stability and consistency. This formulation is then solved in time with the help of an explicit Runge-Kutta time stepping scheme (chapter \ref{sec:TimeStep}).

\subsection{Limiter}
In order to obtain accurate and stable simulations, two limiters are introduced, both of which utilize a first-order $L^2$-projection of $u_h$. To implement this projection, we employ a least-squares approach that remains as close as possible to the original numerical solution, thereby ensuring that the projection remains approximately mass-conserving.
\subsubsection{First-Order Slope Limiter}
The general formulation of the first-order slope-limited approximation is given by
\begin{equation*}
	u_h|_{I_i} = \bar{u}_i + (x - x_i) \sigma_{x,i}, \quad i = 1, 2, \ldots, N_{\text{cells}},
\end{equation*}
where $\sigma_{x,i}$ is the slope, and $\bar{u}_i$ is the average solution in cell $I_i$. Using the minmod slope limiter proposed by Osher \cite{OsherLimiter} with a slight modification where we accept values under a certain threshold:
\begin{equation*}
	\Lambda \Pi^1_h u_h |_{I_i} = \bar{u}_i + (x - x_i) \bar{m} \left( u_{x,i}, \frac{\bar{u}_i - \bar{u}_{i-1}}{h_i/2}, \frac{\bar{u}_{i+1} - \bar{u}_i}{h_i/2} \right),
\end{equation*}
where
\begin{equation*}
	\bar{m}(a_1, a_2, a_3) = 
	\begin{cases}
		a_1, & \text{if } |a_1| \leq \mu h^2, \\
		s \min(|a_1|, |a_2|, |a_3|), & \text{if } |a_1| > \mu h^2 \text{ and } s = \operatorname{sgn}(a_k),\ k = 1,2,3, \\
		0, & \text{otherwise},
	\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
here $\mu\geq 0$ has to be chosen in such a way that the total variation boundedness (TVB) and high order accuracy is ensured, for this work only $\mu=1$ was used.
The limiter is applied in such a way that it preserves the current boundary values of the solution unless their deviation from the cell average is too large. This enforcement is given by:
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{aligned}
		\Lambda \Pi^1_h u_{h, i+1/2}^{-} &= \bar{u}_i + \bar{m}(u_{i+1/2}^{-} - \bar{u}_i, \bar{u}_i - \bar{u}_{i-1}, \bar{u}_{i+1} - \bar{u}_i), \\
		\Lambda \Pi^1_h u_{h, i-1/2}^{+} &= \bar{u}_i - \bar{m}(\bar{u}_i - u_{i-1/2}^{+}, \bar{u}_i - \bar{u}_{i-1}, \bar{u}_{i+1} - \bar{u}_i).
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
If the following conditions hold:
\[
\Lambda \Pi^1_h u_{h, i+1/2}^{-} = u_{i+1/2}^{-} \quad \text{and} \quad \Lambda \Pi^1_h u_{h, i-1/2}^{+} = u_{i-1/2}^{+},
\]
then no limiting is needed. Otherwise, a local first-order $L^2$-projection $u_h^1$ of $u_h$ is computed, and the limiter $\Lambda \Pi^1$ is reapplied.

\subsubsection{Second Limiter: Positivity Preservation}

The slope limiter $ \Lambda\Pi^1 $ alone does not guarantee non-negativity within the cell, which is essential due to the presence of square roots in the LDG formulation. To enforce positivity, a second limiting step is introduced:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item If any value in a cell is negative, compute a local first-order $L^2$-projection $u_h^1$ of $u_h$.
	\item Calculate the average value $\bar{u}_i$ and apply the second limiter $P\Pi u_h^1$.
\end{enumerate}
This second limiter reconstructs a linear function based on the cell average to ensure positivity throughout the cell
\begin{equation*}
	P \Pi^1_h u_h^1 = 
	\begin{cases}
		\left[1 - \frac{2(x - x_i)}{h_i} \right] \bar{u}_i, & \text{if } u_{h, i+1/2}^{-} < 0, \\
		\left[1 + \frac{2(x - x_i)}{h_i} \right] \bar{u}_i, & \text{if } u_{h, i-1/2}^{+} < 0.
	\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
Assuming only one interface value is negative, which numerical experiments in chapter \ref{sec:numerical-results} verify, we make use of the midpoint integration rule after the first-order $ L^2$-projection was done with
\[
\bar{u}_i = \frac{1}{2} \left( u_{i+1/2}^{-} + u_{i-1/2}^{+} \right),
\]
which preserves the cell average and ensures
\[
2|\bar{u}_i| < u_{i-1/2}^{+} \quad \text{or} \quad 2|\bar{u}_i| < u_{i+1/2}^{-}.
\] 
This guarantees that the reconstruction does not introduce new extrema. Since the negative interface value is clamped to zero, the total variation does not increase, thus fulfilling the TVB (Total Variation Bound) property.
As a result, this linear reconstruction guarantees that the solution remains bounded, continuous, and positive within the cell. The reconstructed function takes the explicit form
\begin{equation*}
	u^1_h(x) = \left( u_{i+1/2}^{-} - u_{i-1/2}^{+} \right) \frac{x - x_i}{h_i} + \frac{1}{2} \left( u_{i+1/2}^{-} + u_{i-1/2}^{+} \right).
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Handling Negative Cell Averages}
While Zhang and Wu \cite{Zhang2009} showed that their method avoids negative cell averages for polynomial degree $l = 0$, the numerical experiments provided in this work indicate this does not always hold in practice for varying polynomial degrees. Therefore, as a final safeguard:
\begin{equation*}
	P \Pi^1_h u_h^1 = 
	\begin{cases}
		\left[1 - \frac{2(x - x_i)}{h_i} \right] \bar{u}_i, & \text{if } u_{h, i+1/2}^{-} < 0, \\
		\left[1 + \frac{2(x - x_i)}{h_i} \right] \bar{u}_i, & \text{if } u_{h, i-1/2}^{+} < 0, \\
		0, & \text{if } \bar{u}_i < 0,
	\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
is used to avoid such cases.

\subsection{Time-stepping}\label{sec:TimeStep}
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------%
For time discretization of the semi-discrete Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) formulation, a third-order explicit Runge-Kutta scheme is utilized. The spatial DG scheme (Eq.:\ref{eq:semidiscreteLDG} and Eq.:\ref{eq:semidiscreteHP}) can be written as a first-order ODE system
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d} u_h}{\mathrm{d} t} = \mathbb{M}^{-1} \operatorname{RHS}(u_h) = \mathcal{L}_h(u_h), \quad t \in (0, T),
\]
where \(\mathbb{M}\) is the mass matrix and \(\operatorname{RHS}(u_h)\) represents the spatial DG discretization operator.
The third-order Runge-Kutta update from time \( t^n \) to \( t^{n+1} = t^n + \Delta t^n \) is then given by
\[
\begin{aligned}
	u^{(1)} &= u_h^n + \Delta t^n \, \mathcal{L}_h\big(u_h^n\big), \\
	u^{(2)} &= \frac{3}{4} u_h^n + \frac{1}{4} u^{(1)} + \frac{1}{4} \Delta t^n \, \mathcal{L}_h\big(u^{(1)}\big), \\
	u_h^{n+1} &= \frac{1}{3} u_h^n + \frac{2}{3} u^{(2)} + \frac{2}{3} \Delta t^n \, \mathcal{L}_h\big(u^{(2)}\big).
\end{aligned}
\]
The time step \( \Delta t^n \) must satisfy appropriate stability constraints, which differ depending on the numerical scheme. These conditions are expressed as\\
\begin{align}
	\text{LDG:} \quad
	\frac{\max |f'(u_h^n)| \, \Delta t^n}{\Delta x} + \frac{2 \max |a(u_h^n)| \, \Delta t^n}{(\Delta x)^2} &\leq \textit{CFL} \cdot \frac{1}{l+ 1}, \\
	\text{Hyperbolic system:} \quad
	\frac{\max |u_h^n| \, \Delta t^n}{\Delta x \sqrt{\varepsilon}} + \frac{2 \max |j_h^n| \, \Delta t^n}{(\Delta x)^2 \varepsilon} &\leq \textit{CFL} \cdot \frac{1}{l+ 1},
\end{align}
where \( l \) denotes the maximal polynomial degree of the basis functions, and \textit{CFL} is the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy number selected according to stability requirements.\\
In line with Zhang and Wu~\cite{Zhang2009}, a \textit{CFL} number of \( 0.02 \) was adopted for the LDG method with polynomial degree \( l = 2 \), and this value was retained throughout this work even for higher-order polynomials. For the hyperbolic system, a substantially larger value of \( \textit{CFL} = 0.9 \) was found to yield stable results in all tested scenarios.\\
This choice stands in contrast to the more restrictive \textit{CFL} numbers reported by Sigal and Shu~\cite{RKShuSigal}, particularly those presented in their Table 2.1. However, the increased \textit{CFL} value is justified in the current study, since the time step scales with \( \frac{\Delta t}{(\Delta x)^2\varepsilon} \), and stability is maintained provided that the grid spacing satisfies \( \max \Delta x_i < 1 \) for all \( 1 \leq i \leq N_{\text{cells}} \).

\section{Practical Implementation}

All calculations were performed in double precision. Additionally, for polynomial degrees \( l > 15 \), a more accurate algorithm for computing Gauss–Legendre nodes must be implemented.\\

Switching from the LDG formulation to the hyperbolic system is straightforward: it only requires changing the \texttt{FluxFunction} and setting the parameter \( \varepsilon \neq 0 \) and \texttt{FluxFunction}  to anything else but \texttt{ldg}. This flexibility enables the simulation of a wide range of test cases and modifications to the porous medium equation. For example, one can include a source term of the form \( cu^p \), and/or add a transport term, which can be specified via the \texttt{TransportFlux} option.\\

\section{Numerical Results}\label{sec:numerical-results}
All numerical simulations were performed on an Intel i7-3600K processor equipped with 16\,GB of DDR3 RAM. The computational domain was \([-6,6]\), and unless stated otherwise, all error calculations were conducted on the subdomain \([-1.5,1.5]\). For both numerical approaches (LDG/hyperbolic system) periodic boundary conditions were imposed.
\paragraph{Local Discontinuous Galerkin}  
To initiate the simulation, one must select the artificial diffusion parameter \(\gamma\). Zhang and Shu~\cite{Zhang2009} proposed the following formulation:
\[
\gamma_{j+\frac{1}{2}} =
\begin{cases}
	\frac{1}{2} \dfrac{g_{j+1} - g_j}{u_{j+1} - u_j}, & \text{if } u_{j+1} \neq u_j, \\[10pt]
	\frac{1}{2} \sqrt{a(u_{h,j})}, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
This approach has been analytically justified and is suggested to extend naturally to higher-order polynomial basis functions beyond the case of polynomial degree \(l=0\). In the following numerical experiments, no stability issues were encountered, even for high exponents such as \(m=8\) coupled with a high order polynomial like $l=5$.\\
\\
\textit{Case 1 } For the first numerical experiment the Barenblatt solution~(Eq.:\ref{eq:barenblatt function}) was used as initial condition at \(t=1\,\mathrm{s}\). As expected the numerical experiments show that the accuracy of the numerical scheme appears to heavily depend on the nonlinearity.
Only for the odd exponents (\(m=1,~3,~5,~7\)), the numbers of limiter calls are in comparison rather low, and the experimental order of convergence (EOC) is maintained.
In contrast, for all even exponents (e.g $m=2,~4,~6,~8$), the numbers of limiter calls increase substantially, compare Tab.:\ref{table:BBM2P2} and Tab.:\ref{table:BBM3P2}, and no improvement in accuracy was observed with \(h\)-refinement.

However, it should be noted that their approach disregarded the entire computational domain and instead imposed Dirichlet boundary conditions, computing errors only within the arch region and excluding areas containing kinks. Specifically, they focused solely on the domain \([-1.5, 1.5]\) with Dirichlet boundaries. precision coupled with the adaptive threshold for floating-point comparisons in this work.\\

\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \left(u^m\right)_{x x} - c u^p,
\end{equation}\\

\begin{equation}
	u_0(x)= \begin{cases}|\sin x|, & x \in(-\pi,-\pi / 6) \cup(\pi / 6, \pi), \\ 0.5, & x \in(-\pi / 6, \pi / 6), \\ 0, & \text { otherwise, }

\end{equation}\\

\paragraph{Hyperbolic System}
All numerical experiments for the hyperbolic system were conducted on the spatial domain \( [-6, 6] \) using a uniform mesh with \( N_{\text{cells}} = 320 \), unless stated otherwise. The Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) number was set to \( \textit{CFL} = 0.9 \), and a central numerical flux was used to compute interface values at cell boundaries. The artificial parameter \( \varepsilon = 0.01 \) was selected to investigate the behavior of the porous medium equation, unless otherwise specified. Only for the experiment of varying $\varepsilon$ the polynomial degree of $l=2$ was chosen. For all other tests the choice was $l=3$.\\
\\
\textit{Case 1} We consider the initial data
\[
u_0(x) = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2}\right),
\label{eq: smoothInitialExp}
\]
which is smooth and compactly supported on the computational domain. The goal of this experiment is to examine whether the solution of the hyperbolic system converges to that of the porous medium equation as \( \varepsilon \to 0 \).
Fig.~\ref{fig:gam8pp2central1s} illustrates the behavior of the solution for \( m = 1 \) under varying values of \( \varepsilon \). As \( \varepsilon \) decreases, the numerical solution converges toward the expected result. Remarkably, even for a relatively high \( \varepsilon = 0.01 \), the approximation exhibits good accuracy, and further reductions in \( \varepsilon \) produce only marginal improvements. A comparison to the LDG formulation is also provided in Fig.~\ref{fig:gam8pp2central1s} that clearly shows that the choice of $\varepsilon=0.01$ is reasonable, since we know that the LDG formulation is relatively exact for $m=1$.\\
For \( m = 1 \), the diffusion corresponds to the classical heat equation, which yields smooth solutions for all \( t > 0 \). As a result, no limiters were applied in this case. However, increasing \( m \) introduces nonlinear diffusion effects, which lead to the formation of steep gradients in the solution after a certain time \( t > 0 \). To address this, the two previously introduced limiters were employed. Specifically, if the first limiter (minmod) flags a troubled cell, the two neighboring cells are also limited by projecting their solutions to first-order polynomials. This conservative strategy enhances stability and helps reduce oscillations.
Despite this aggressive limiting, substantial oscillations still occur for larger nonlinearities such as \( m = 6, 7, 8 \). In contrast, for smaller values of \( m \), the solution remains mostly free of spurious oscillations when starting from smooth initial data (compare Fig.~\ref{fig:HGAVariousM}).\\

	\textit{Case 4} The final test case aims to investigate whether the hyperbolic system is capable of representing the waiting time phenomenon. For this purpose, the following specially designed initial condition is used\\
\end{flushleft}

\begin{equation*}
	u_0(x)= \begin{cases}
		\cos x, & \text{if } x \in\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right), \\
		0, & \text{otherwise}.
	\end{cases}
\end{equation*}\\
At early times \( t > 0\,\text{s} \), when the solution remains close to the initial data, the hyperbolic system successfully reproduces the waiting time phenomenon, even when using a relatively small parameter value of \( \varepsilon = 0.01 \) (see Fig.~\ref{fig:HWaitingVariousM}). However, as time progresses, small oscillations begin to appear despite the strong enforcement of the modified limiters. These oscillations grow in magnitude over time but remain localized near the kinks in the solution.\\
Despite these oscillations, the numerical solution appears to capture the qualitative behavior of the porous medium equation (PME) accurately and is capable of producing sharp transitions at the free boundary. This case is particularly noteworthy due to the high nonlinearity exponent \( m = 8 \). In comparison, the hyperbolic system was unable to accurately capture the sharp free boundary for the Barenblatt initial data with the same exponent at \( t = 1\,\text{s} \) (compare Fig.~\ref{fig:hbbm8}), which supports the observation that the choice of initial data plays a crucial role in the accuracy and stability of the simulation.

\section{Conclusion}
In this research project a numerical investigation of the porous medium equation (PME) using two distinct spatial discretization strategies were presented, the Local Discontinuous Galerkin (LDG) method and a hyperbolic balance law (HBL) formulation. The implementation emphasizes critical numerical properties, including preservation of positivity, suppression of spurious oscillations and adaptive tolerance thresholds for identifying nearly equal values. These features contribute to maintaining numerical stability as well as physical accuracy, particularly in degenerate regimes.
The numerical results show that the LDG method, when equipped with carefully tuned limiters, provides accurate and robust solutions over a broad range of nonlinearity exponents $m$ and polynomial degrees $ l $. Although certain difficulties remain for extended simulation times, especially in accurately depicting both the free boundary and smooth interior regions, the LDG approach successfully captures essential features of the PME, such as the waiting time phenomenon, nonlinear diffusion and the splitting of the support region.
By comparison, the HBL formulation exhibits reduced robustness with respect to oscillatory behavior.It still captures essential qualitative behaviors of the PME, including nonlinear diffusion effects and waiting time dynamics, even with relatively large values of the relaxation parameter $\varepsilon$. However, the divorce of the support region cannot be observed in the corresponding test case. Additionally, the use of non-smooth initial data, such as the Barenblatt function, led to significant oscillations near points where the initial profile lacks continuous differentiability. In such cases, the application of a stricter limiter proved effective in controlling these oscillations.
A detailed analysis of convergence behavior, limiter activity, and solution structure highlights the influence of initial data selection and boundary treatment on the accuracy and stability of the HBL system. In particular, discrepancies in the observed experimental orders of convergence for the LDG method, when compared with previous studies such as those by Zhang and Wu \cite{Zhang2009}, are primarily attributed to the full-domain computation strategy employed in this work.\\
In the future one might introduce a remeshing procedure coupled with mesh adaptivity to the LDG scheme to improve the accuracy and mass conservation. Furthermore this may alleviate the problem of occurring negative values, reducing the number of $ P\Pi_h $ limiter calls are necessary. Also an extension to 2D would be interesting.
